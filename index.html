Your phone doesn't have an atomic clock.

So how does it know how long a GPS signal took to arrive?

1 · The problem

Each satellite sends out the time. Your phone measures how long the signal took and turns it into a distance: a circle around that satellite. Where the circles meet is you.

But light travels 300 m in one microsecond, and your phone's clock is a cheap quartz crystal. Drag the slider:

2 · The trick

Your phone's clock is wrong by the same amount for every satellite. So every circle is too big by the same amount.

Shrink them all together. When they meet at one point, you've found the clock error, and your position with it.

corrected distance = measured − (speed of light × your guess)

3 · How it's computed

A computer doesn't try every value. It starts from a guess (pink dot) and improves it.

Each satellite compares the distance it measured with the distance the guess predicts, and pushes: outward if measured is longer, inward if shorter. Least squares combines the pushes into one step (dashed pink).

If every arrow pushes outward by the same amount, it isn't your position that's wrong. It's the clock.

measured = √((x−xᵢ)² + (y−yᵢ)²) + c × clock error
